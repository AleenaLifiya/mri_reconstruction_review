Putting the Slices Together: A Review on Image-Domain MRI Reconstruction and Super-Resolution

Aleena Lifiya Jeffy1,2, Mohammed Q. Alkhatib1, Marouane Tliba3, Aladine Chetouani3, Nour Aburaed1

1College of Engineering and IT, University of Dubai, Dubai, United Arab Emirates
2American University of Sharjah, Sharjah, United Arab Emirates
3L2TI, Université Sorbonne Paris Nord, Villetaneuse, France

Abstract

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) depicts soft tissue with high contrast and without ionizing radiation, which makes it central to diagnosis and quantitative anatomical studies. Acquisition is slow, however, because spatial resolution, signal-to-noise ratio and scan time are coupled, and longer scans are more exposed to patient motion. Clinical protocols therefore shorten scans by undersampling or acquiring thick, widely spaced slices, which leaves volumes degraded and anisotropic. Image-domain reconstruction and super-resolution estimate the missing information after a shorter acquisition, and some methods also correct motion between slices. This review surveys such methods published from 2020 to 2026, selecting 181 of 452 records from four sources, reported with a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 flow diagram. It defines six reconstruction settings with one forward model and classifies every study by setting and by whether its degraded input is simulated or acquired. Beyond a taxonomy of classical interpolation and seven deep learning families, it examines public datasets and their populations, reported image quality, validation through downstream tasks, clinical readers, anatomical measurements and uncertainty, and computational cost. The analysis shows that through-plane settings receive less attention than in-plane ones and most evidence rests on simulated degradation and image-quality scores. The review closes with a reporting checklist and research directions, including the role of agentic artificial intelligence, to move these methods toward clinical confidence.

1Why it matters

MRI depicts soft tissue with high contrast and without ionizing radiation, but a high-resolution scan takes time, and longer scans are more exposed to patient motion. Clinical protocols therefore shorten scans by undersampling or by acquiring thick, widely spaced slices. Image-domain reconstruction and super-resolution estimate the missing information after the shorter acquisition.

Main finding. Through-plane settings match the thick or widely spaced slices of clinical protocols, yet 32 of the 181 works address them, against 92 for in-plane super-resolution. Most of the evidence rests on simulated degradation and image-quality scores.

2How the studies were selected

The search covered Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, Springer Nature Link and the ACM Digital Library, with citation searching, for works published from 2020 to 2026. The procedure is adapted from the PRISMA 2020 statement. Included works use a publicly available MRI dataset and an image-domain or hybrid image and k-space method.

3Six reconstruction settings

One forward model describes the acquired volume X as a degraded version of the dense volume Y, and a model estimates Y from it:

X = Hδ(Y),    Ŷ = fθ(X)

A setting is defined by which operators in Hδ are active. The settings remove different information, so progress in one does not carry over to another by default. Select a setting to filter the matrix.

Where the degraded input comes from

Only 29 works test a degraded input from a real acquisition. For most works, performance on acquired data is untested.

4Datasets

The 181 works use 40 public datasets. Five of them (IXI, fastMRI Brain, the BraTS family, fastMRI Knee and the Human Connectome Project) account for 73 % of the 290 paper-dataset associations, and 15 datasets are used by exactly one work. The cohorts behind them are mostly healthy or undocumented adults.

5Models

The review covers classical interpolation and seven deep learning families. The field has moved from convolutional and adversarial networks to Transformers, diffusion models, implicit neural representations, deep unfolding and state-space models, and recent designs increasingly combine them. Select a family to filter the matrix.

    6What the evidence shows

    Reported scores cannot be ranked across studies, because degradations, data partitions, normalization and metric implementations differ.

    Within each study, the results show what a method achieves under the conditions tested and how its performance falls as slices become sparser. The evidence does not yet show that a reconstructed slice can stand in for an acquired one in diagnosis. Every slice at an unacquired position is an estimate, and a lesion that lies entirely between acquired slices cannot be recovered by any model.

    7What would move the field

    1. Shared benchmarks with real paired acquisitions, with fixed partitions and shared metric code.
    2. Evaluation that follows the clinical question, counting missed and false findings.
    3. Reconstructions that state a calibrated confidence and can be checked without a reference.
    4. Complete reporting, including end-to-end reconstruction time on stated hardware.
    Reporting checklist for studies of MRI slice reconstruction and super-resolution.
    TopicWhat to report
    SettingThe reconstruction setting and active degradation operators, the factor and its kind, and results at several factors
    Degraded inputSimulated or acquired, the full simulated operator, and at least one test on acquired data where the setting allows it
    DataDataset and release, cohort, an explicit train, validation and test assignment, and results per dataset or organ
    Image qualityMasked and unmasked scores, normalization and data range, metric library and parameters, 2D or 3D evaluation, and aggregation
    ValidationReader or downstream-task evidence for any clinical claim, with fixed task models and reader study design
    UncertaintyThe uncertainty component, target, sampling, calibration or error criterion, and interval coverage with width
    Computational costParameters, operation counts, end-to-end time per volume and memory on stated hardware, throughput and, where possible, energy
    ReproducibilityCode, trained weights and access to the data
