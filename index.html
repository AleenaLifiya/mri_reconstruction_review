Putting the Slices Together: A Review on Image-Domain MRI Reconstruction and Super-Resolution

Aleena Lifiya Jeffy1,2, Mohammed Q. Alkhatib1, Marouane Tliba3, Aladine Chetouani3, Nour Aburaed1

1College of Engineering and IT, University of Dubai, Dubai, United Arab Emirates
2American University of Sharjah, Sharjah, United Arab Emirates
3L2TI, Université Sorbonne Paris Nord, Villetaneuse, France

Abstract

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) depicts soft tissue with high contrast and without ionizing radiation, which makes it central to diagnosis and quantitative anatomical studies. Acquisition is slow, however, because spatial resolution, signal-to-noise ratio and scan time are coupled, and longer scans are more exposed to patient motion. Clinical protocols therefore shorten scans by undersampling or acquiring thick, widely spaced slices, which leaves volumes degraded and anisotropic. Image-domain reconstruction and super-resolution estimate the missing information after a shorter acquisition, and some methods also correct motion between slices. This review surveys such methods published from 2020 to 2026, selecting 181 of 452 records from four sources, reported with a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 flow diagram. It defines six reconstruction settings with one forward model and classifies every study by setting and by whether its degraded input is simulated or acquired. Beyond a taxonomy of classical interpolation and seven deep learning families, it examines public datasets and their populations, reported image quality, validation through downstream tasks, clinical readers, anatomical measurements and uncertainty, and computational cost. The analysis shows that through-plane settings receive less attention than in-plane ones and most evidence rests on simulated degradation and image-quality scores. The review closes with a reporting checklist and research directions, including the role of agentic artificial intelligence, to move these methods toward clinical confidence.

A summary of the review, section by section. Section numbers match the manuscript.

1Introduction

MRI depicts soft tissue with high contrast and without ionizing radiation, but spatial resolution, signal-to-noise ratio and scan time are coupled, and longer scans are more exposed to patient motion. Clinical protocols therefore shorten scans by undersampling or by acquiring thick, widely spaced slices. Image-domain reconstruction and super-resolution estimate the missing information after the shorter acquisition.

Main finding. Through-plane settings match the thick or widely spaced slices of clinical protocols, yet 32 of the 181 works address them, against 92 for in-plane super-resolution. Most of the evidence rests on simulated degradation and image-quality scores.

What the review contributes

  • A systematic corpus of 181 works, classified by setting, source of the degraded input and supervision.
  • An inventory of 40 datasets and a taxonomy of classical interpolation and seven deep learning families.
  • An analysis of reported results: which scores can be compared and how methods behave across datasets and organs.
  • A synthesis of validation through downstream tasks, clinical readers, anatomical measurements and uncertainty.
  • An account of computational cost and code availability, with a reporting checklist.

2Literature search and selection

The search covered Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, Springer Nature Link and the ACM Digital Library, with citation searching, for works published from 2020 to 2026. The procedure is adapted from the PRISMA 2020 statement. Included works use a publicly available MRI dataset and an image-domain or hybrid image and k-space method.

3Problem definition

One forward model describes the acquired volume X as a degraded version of the dense volume Y, and a reconstruction model estimates Y from it:

X = Hδ(Y),    Ŷ = fθ(X)

The degradation is a chain of four steps: the position of the anatomy, the formation of each slice, the measurement in k-space, and the scanner's reconstruction. A setting is defined by which operators in this chain are active. Select a setting to filter the matrix.

A factor r states how much is removed. With 12 dense positions and every fourth one acquired, r = 4 and 75 % of the positions are unobserved. Equal factors in different settings can differ in difficulty, because they remove different information. The problem is ill-posed, since many dense volumes are consistent with one input, so whatever a model returns beyond the measurements is an estimate.

Where the degraded input comes from

Most works degrade released magnitude images. 137 use simulated inputs only, 25 use simulated and real inputs, and 4 use real inputs only. Real acquisitions enter 13 of the 32 through-plane works, 12 of the 92 in-plane works and 4 of the 47 accelerated works.

The simulated operator also varies. Among the in-plane works, 44 truncate central k-space, 19 downsample bicubically, 11 blur before downsampling, 3 learn the degradation and 22 do not state it. Two works reporting the same factor may therefore have removed different information.

4Preliminaries

This section follows an MR volume from the scanner to the input of a model.

  • Scanner and contrast. Most scanners and reviewed datasets operate at 1.5 T or 3 T. Low-field scanners are cheaper but noisier and often use thick slices. Sequence timing sets the contrast, and multi-contrast methods use one contrast to guide another.
  • Slice geometry. Thickness is the width of tissue a slice is acquired from, spacing is the distance between slice centres, and the gap is spacing minus thickness. Thick slices blur structure along the stacking axis, and gaps leave tissue unsampled.
  • Storage. DICOM keeps slice thickness and position with each slice. NIfTI stores voxel spacing and orientation without slice thickness, so gaps cannot be detected after conversion.
  • Preprocessing. Bias-field correction, skull stripping, registration, resampling, cropping and normalization each change what a model is asked to reconstruct or how its output is scored.

5Datasets

The 181 works use 40 public datasets. Five of them (IXI, fastMRI Brain, the BraTS family, fastMRI Knee and the Human Connectome Project) account for 73 % of the 290 paper-dataset associations, and 15 datasets are used by exactly one work. Twenty-three datasets are brain datasets, used by 150 works, against 5 datasets and 39 works for the knee and 4 datasets and 8 works for the heart.

  • Cohort documentation. For 99 of the 290 associations, the release does not describe its cohort.
  • Health status. Of the other 191, 59 % fall on healthy-volunteer cohorts and 39 % on disease-defined cohorts, half of which are glioma.
  • Geography. At least 229 associations (79 %) come from North America or Europe, and no dataset comes from Africa or South America.
  • Supervision. 155 works use paired training, and for 81.8 % of all works it is the only training signal. It needs a reference for every degraded input, which ties it to simulated data.

6Models

Classical interpolation (nearest-neighbour, linear and cubic) applies a fixed rule, needs no training and remains the standard baseline. It can densify the sampling grid, but it cannot restore information the acquisition did not retain. Deep learning instead learns an anatomical prior from examples, from the scan itself or from an acquisition model. Select a family to filter the matrix.

The field has moved from convolutional and adversarial networks to Transformers, diffusion, implicit representations, unfolding and state-space models. Recent designs assign local fidelity, long-range context, a generative or continuous prior and data consistency to separate components of one model.

7Evaluation

PSNR aggregates the error of every voxel, SSIM the agreement of local structure, and LPIPS the distance between learned features. Masked and unmasked scores can differ considerably, so a score needs its region stated.

Absolute scores cannot be compared across studies, because subjects, degradation operators, normalization and metric implementations differ. The rate at which a method degrades is more informative, and it depends on the data: HocMRI loses 3.51 dB per doubling of the factor on IXI, 1.56 dB on M4Raw and 0.97 dB on fastMRI.

76 works evaluate on two or more datasets. Of these, 52 stay within one organ, 48 of them in the brain, and 24 span several anatomies. Within a study, PSNR is higher for the brain than the knee in 14 of 17 usable pairs, and SSIM in all 16 that report it.

8Validation

Agreement with a reference is necessary evidence, and it leaves three questions open: whether a small lesion or boundary is preserved, whether a clinical or quantitative decision would change, and how reliable an output is when no reference exists. The review separates four kinds of evidence.

10downstream-task studies

Test whether the structures or pathology an analysis needs survive, most often through segmentation.

3reader studies

Score diagnostic quality or separate real from generated volumes. None counts missed or false findings against a reference.

5anatomical validations

Measure error around clinical targets, or compare surfaces, volumes and other extracted measurements.

7uncertainty studies

Estimate or use predictive uncertainty. The estimates describe different quantities and cannot be ranked on one scale.

9Computational efficiency

A shorter scan frees the scanner whatever the reconstruction time, but a slow reconstruction delays the images when they are needed at once and limits throughput across many examinations.

Only 29 works (16.0 %) report any computational value: 20 the number of parameters, 18 an operation count, 15 a time or throughput, 2 the memory use and 1 the number of function evaluations. They use different inputs, devices and counting conventions. On the reported devices, feed-forward slice methods span tens to hundreds of milliseconds, while iterative diffusion methods take 45 s per slice in one study and about 141 s per 3D volume in another.

10Discussion

Read together, the analyses point to limitations that the reviewed works share. The chart counts how many of the 181 works have each property that a convincing evaluation needs.

A score depends on its protocol

In the review's sensitivity analysis, one fixed BraTS reconstruction scored from 26.01 to 33.21 dB and from 0.785 to 0.948 in SSIM as the region, normalization, implementation and slice averaging changed. An unstated rule for averaging slices can also reverse the ranking of two methods.

Generated slices need stated confidence

A hallucination is a plausible, incorrect structure that the prior adds or removes. Degrading the output and comparing it with the input tests only what was measured, so a missed lesion between acquired slices passes this check. Among the reviewed works, only X-Diffusion formulates conformal prediction intervals.

How sparse is too sparse?

With 1 mm slices acquired every 5 mm, no acquired slice touches a 2 mm lesion in 40 % of positions. In contiguous 5 mm slices, the same lesion keeps only 20 to 40 % of its contrast. An acquisition with gaps is too sparse when the gap exceeds the smallest finding the scan must not miss.

Agentic AI

Agentic systems can automate parts of the pathway, but no current check can establish that generated content is true for the individual patient. Until the evidence exists, their appropriate role is a supervised control layer around validated models, with the clinician responsible for the reading.

Limitations of this review

The review covers four sources and works published in English from 2020 to 2026. Works that rely on private data only were excluded so that every result refers to accessible data, which also removes some in-house paired acquisitions.

11Conclusion

Within each study, the results show what a method achieves under the conditions tested and how its performance falls as slices become sparser. The evidence does not yet show that a reconstructed slice can stand in for an acquired one in diagnosis. Every slice at an unacquired position is an estimate, and a lesion that lies entirely between acquired slices cannot be recovered by any model. Four actions would move the field furthest:

  1. Shared benchmarks with real paired acquisitions, with fixed partitions and shared metric code.
  2. Evaluation that follows the clinical question, counting missed and false findings.
  3. Reconstructions that state a calibrated confidence and can be checked without a reference.
  4. Complete reporting, including end-to-end reconstruction time on stated hardware.
